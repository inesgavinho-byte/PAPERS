We live in an economy organised to capture attention, and most of the built environment has quietly enlisted in it. The screen, the notification, the engineered distraction — and the spaces that house them, designed to keep us moving, spending, and never quite present. Against this, a principle.

**A space should return a person to themselves.**

Mental sovereignty is the inhabitant's command over their own interior life — the freedom to attend to what they choose, to think without interruption, to be wholly present rather than perpetually solicited. It is the scarcest freedom of the age, and architecture is among the few instruments powerful enough to protect or to erode it.

Three of these Papers converge on this commitment from different directions. *On Inhabited Time* argues that a space which holds duration gives back the slow time in which thought becomes possible. *The Architecture of the Interval* defends the pause as the condition of presence — the rest without which experience cannot compose itself. *A New Spatial Humanism* asks that a room answer the whole interior life rather than starve or distract it.

To design for mental sovereignty is to ask, of every space: does this return the person to themselves, or does it take something from them? Does it offer quiet, duration, and a place for attention to rest — or does it hurry, fragment, and solicit? A sovereign space is not empty and it is not silent in the literal sense. It is *uncolonised*. It leaves the inhabitant in possession of their own mind.
