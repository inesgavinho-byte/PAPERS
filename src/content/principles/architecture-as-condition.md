The deepest error in how we talk about buildings is to call them objects. An object is finished, owned, and bounded; its effects stop at its edges. A building does none of these things. It is entered — by inhabitants, by neighbours, by strangers, by the future — and its effects continue for as long as it stands. It is not a thing. It is a condition.

**A building sets the terms on which a shared life is conducted.**

To treat architecture as condition rather than object changes who the work is for and how long the responsibility lasts. *The Architectural Project of We* draws out the first consequence: if a building is a condition, then it is authored on behalf of everyone who will inhabit it, including the stranger who never signed the brief. *The Appearance and the Function* draws out the second: if a building is a condition, then its value lies in what it makes possible for a life, not in the novelty of its image — function, not appearance, is the measure.

A condition cannot be photographed and it is never simply complete. It is tested daily by use, extended by maintenance, and judged over decades by people the architect will never meet. To design under this principle is to accept the wider authorship it implies: to ask not what the object looks like on its opening day, but what condition it will have set for the shared life that follows — and to be answerable for the answer.
