Consider the space you pass through without noticing: the lobby, the landing, the corridor, the porch. The brief calls it circulation. The cost plan calls it inefficiency. The optimiser calls it waste — area that serves no programme, time spent not yet arrived. The entire instinct of modern planning is to compress it toward zero.

I want to argue that this instinct, pursued faithfully, hollows out the very experience it means to deliver.

## The assumption of the shortest path

Behind the war on circulation lies a model of human movement borrowed from logistics: a person is a parcel, and the ideal route delivers the parcel to its destination with minimum delay. Under this model, the space between two rooms has no value of its own. It is pure cost — a transition, a means, something to be got through.

But people are not parcels, and the in-between is not dead time. The threshold where you pause before entering a room; the half-landing where the stair turns and offers a view; the porch where you stand a moment before knocking — these are not failures of efficiency. They are where the mind catches up with the body. They are where you become ready to arrive.

Remove them and you do not save time. You abolish the conditions for presence.

## Transition and interval

Here is the distinction. A *transition* is instrumental: it exists to be crossed, and its ideal length is none. An *interval* is generative: it exists to be dwelt in, however briefly, and its length is exactly what the experience requires.

Music knows this perfectly. A rest is not silence between notes; it is a note. Remove the rests and the melody does not get faster — it ceases to be music and becomes noise. The interval *is* the rhythm. The same is true of a building: the pauses are not the gaps in the experience but its meter.

> The interval is to architecture what the rest is to music: not the absence of the thing, but the condition of its being heard.

The Japanese language carries a word for this that ours lacks. *Ma* (間) names the charged emptiness between things — the interval of space or time that gives the things their relation and their meaning.[^ma] In a culture attuned to *ma*, the pause is not a deficiency to be removed but a presence to be shaped. The space between two pillars, the silence between two utterances, the moment between two events: each is composed, not endured.

[^ma]: *Ma* (間) resists direct translation: "interval," "gap," "pause," "the space between." It treats emptiness as relational and active — the negative space that lets the positive be perceived. A room with *ma* is not under-furnished; it is correctly weighted.

## The thesis: the interval composes experience

So the interval is not a gap in experience. It is the faculty by which experience is composed. We do not feel arrival without a threshold to have crossed; we do not feel sanctuary without a passage that prepared us for it; we do not feel that we belong somewhere without the ceremony, however small, of having been received. Aldo van Eyck made the threshold his life's subject precisely because the doorstep is where two worlds are reconciled — and reconciliation takes a moment that the optimiser would delete.[^vaneyck]

[^vaneyck]: For van Eyck the in-between place was where opposites — inside and outside, house and city, individual and collective — could be held together rather than merely adjoined. The width of a doorstep is a measure of how seriously a building takes that meeting.

Strip the intervals out and the building delivers you everywhere and lets you arrive nowhere. You move through it at the speed of logistics, and at that speed there is no presence — only transit. Efficiency, pursued past a point, does not intensify experience. It evacuates it.

## The operational principle

To honour the interval is not to pad a building with wasted space. It is to recognise that some of its most important rooms have no programme — that the pause, the threshold, the slow turn of a stair are load-bearing for the *experience* even when they bear no function on the schedule.

The principle:

**Design the pause as carefully as the destination.**

Give arrival a threshold to cross. Let a passage prepare the room it leads to. Treat the in-between not as cost but as composition — the rests that turn a sequence of spaces into a piece of architecture. What you remove to save time, you may be removing to save presence. Do not abolish the interval. Compose it.
