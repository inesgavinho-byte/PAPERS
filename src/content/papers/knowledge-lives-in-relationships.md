Organisations do not lose knowledge because documents disappear. They lose knowledge because relationships are never made explicit.

We tend to imagine knowledge as a stock — a library of files, a wiki, a folder of procedures. But the most valuable knowledge in any organisation is not stored. It is enacted, in the space between people: who to ask, what a phrase really means, why an exception was once made.

## The relational substrate

Consider what an experienced colleague actually knows. Not facts — those are cheap and increasingly automatable. What they know is *relational*: this supplier is reliable but slow; this clause exists because of a dispute in 2019; this client says yes but means maybe.

None of that lives in a document. It lives in a web of associations between people, events and decisions. When the person leaves, the web does not transfer. It simply goes dark.

> A document records an answer. A relationship records the conditions under which the answer was true.

## Why writing things down is not enough

The reflexive response is *documentation*. Write it down. But documentation captures conclusions, not the reasoning that produced them — and reasoning is what lets an organisation respond to situations it has never seen.

The deeper task is to make relationships explicit:

- between a **decision** and the **evidence** that justified it;
- between a **standard** and the **case** that established it;
- between a **person** and the **judgement** only they currently hold.

## Toward an organisational memory

An organisation that takes this seriously stops treating knowledge as content and starts treating it as a *graph* — a structure of connected reasoning that can be queried, inherited and challenged.

This is slow, unglamorous work. It produces no immediate revenue. But it is the difference between a company that compounds its intelligence and one that quietly resets it every time someone walks out the door.
