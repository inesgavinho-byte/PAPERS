Some organisations keep everything.

Emails.
Messages.
Minutes.
Contracts.
Drawings.
Photographs.
Reports.
Versions.
Comments.
Meeting recordings.

And still they forget.

Not because the information has disappeared.

Because storing is not remembering.

An archive can preserve thousands of documents. Memory needs to know how those documents relate to one another, what happened before and after them, which decisions remained valid and which ceased to be.

The difference sounds small.

It is not.

It is one of the reasons so many projects come to depend on the people who followed them rather than on knowledge the project itself should be able to preserve.

**Record ≠ Memory.**

## A project exists beyond its documents

A project is not a folder.

Nor is it the sum of the files it produced.

A project is a reality that evolves through time.

It has people.
It has decisions.
It has versions.
It has commitments.
It has mistakes.
It has changes.
It has dependencies.
It has things that were planned and never happened.
It has other things that happened without having been planned.

Above all, it has relationships.

When we look at one document in isolation, we see only a partial state of that reality.

The drawing says one thing.

The email explains why it was changed.

The minutes record that someone disagreed.

A later message approves an alternative.

The work on site executes a slightly different solution.

The invoice introduces another piece of evidence.

Months later, someone asks:

**Why did this end up like this?**

And the archaeology begins.

## The permanent reconstruction of context

A large part of contemporary organisational work consists of reconstructing contexts that once existed.

Who made this decision?

Which version was valid?

Was this quotation approved?

Did this change replace the previous one?

Did this person know about it?

Did the supplier deliver what had been specified?

What happened between the decision and the execution?

Usually, the answer exists somewhere.

That is not the problem.

The problem is having to find it again.

Every new person joining a project receives fragments.
Every interruption breaks continuity.
Every change of team requires history to be recovered.
Every dispute requires a sequence of events to be reconstructed.

A vast amount of energy is spent not moving forward, but relearning what the organisation should already know.

## Knowledge trapped inside people

For a long time, we accepted this as inevitable.

There is always someone who “knows”.

The person who remembers that meeting.
The person who understands why a particular decision was made.
The person who knows that a file is not the latest one, despite its name suggesting otherwise.
The person who knows the relationship between two entities the systems treat as unrelated.

These people become the invisible infrastructure of the organisation.

They appear efficient because they can answer quickly.

In fact, they reveal a fragility.

If essential project knowledge exists only because one particular person is still there and remembers it, that knowledge does not truly belong to the organisation.

It belongs to that person's memory.

When they leave, change role, become unavailable or simply forget, part of the organisation disappears with them.

## Continuity is not storage

Technology has tried to solve this by increasing storage capacity.

First there were servers.

Then clouds.

Then drives.

Then document management platforms.

Then project management systems.

Then communication tools.

Now we have artificial intelligence capable of searching through all of them.

But we are still often attacking the wrong problem.

The continuity of a project does not depend only on being able to find an old document.

It depends on being able to understand **its place in the history of the project**.

A decision has a before and an after.

It may supersede another.
It may depend on an approval.
It may be limited to one phase.
It may have been communicated but never executed.
It may have been executed without ever being formally recorded.

A memory system therefore needs to preserve more than objects.

It needs to preserve relationships and time.

## Time is part of the information

A fact without temporality is an incomplete fact.

“It was approved.”

When?

“This is the specification.”

Since when?

“This is the supplier.”

For what period?

“The project specifies this solution.”

In which version?

Much of what we call information is really a statement that is valid only during a particular interval.

Traditional systems tend to store states.

Projects live through transitions.

It was proposed.
It was commented on.
It was rejected.
It was revised.
It was approved.
It was executed.
It was superseded.

The difference between these states is not administrative.

It is knowledge.

An organisation unable to reconstruct that evolution loses an essential part of the reality it is trying to manage.

## The Building Passport as living memory

In architecture and construction, this becomes particularly visible.

A building may last fifty, one hundred or two hundred years.

Much of the information used to build it disappears far sooner.

Designers change.
Contractors finish their contracts.
Owners sell.
Maintenance teams are replaced.
Software disappears.
Formats become obsolete.

The building remains.

Its memory often does not.

We have spoken about *Building Passports* for years, but the idea is often reduced to a digital dossier: materials, certificates, manuals, drawings, equipment.

That is useful.

But it is still an archive.

A true passport for a building should be capable of preserving its history.

What was designed.
What changed.
What was actually built.
Which piece of equipment replaced the previous one.
When an intervention took place.
Why it happened.
Who assumed a particular responsibility.
What evidence supports that information.

Not only **what the building is**.

But **how it became what it is**.

## THREAD emerged from this problem

THREAD began, for me, precisely in this zone of discomfort.

Communications contain a huge part of the reality of a project.

Not only emails.

Messages.
Decisions.
Requests.
Commitments.
Questions.
Changes.
Confirmations.

But communication normally disappears inside the channel that carried it.

An email remains an email.

A message remains a message.

The project does not automatically learn what happened through them.

That is why I began to think about THREAD not as a smarter inbox, but as a layer capable of understanding communication as part of the operational memory of an organisation.

A message matters not only because of its text.

It matters because someone sent it, about something, at a particular moment, in relation to an entity, decision or event.

It matters because it may change what we know.

The objective is therefore no longer to archive communication.

It is to relate communication to the reality it describes.

## DECIMA emerged from the same problem

As I continued working on these questions, it became clear that the problem was larger than communication.

It was not a document problem either.

Nor simply a project problem.

It was a continuity problem.

How can an organisation preserve context over time?

How can it distinguish what happened from what was merely said?

How can it know that one decision superseded another?

How can it preserve provenance?

How can it move across different applications without fragmenting the identity of the people, entities and events they represent?

DECIMA begins with this question.

Not as a document system, but as a system of relationships.

That distinction became fundamental.

A document is an object.

A decision is a relationship.

A project is a network of relationships that changes over time.

So is an organisation.

Once we begin to represent reality in this way, many things stop making sense as independent silos.

Documents should not live detached from people.

People should not exist separately in each application.

Communications should not lose their connection to the decisions they produced.

Artificial-intelligence agents should not operate outside the history that allows them to understand what they are acting upon.

And memory should not depend on a single tool.

## Identity belongs to reality

There is a consequence here that I consider especially important.

If the same person appears in an email system, a CRM, a construction platform and a financial application, we are not dealing with four people.

We are dealing with four representations of the same person.

If a building exists in a document system, maintenance software, a financial application and a BIM model, there are not four buildings either.

There is one reality.

The systems are perspectives on it.

This sounds obvious when stated this way.

But much of the digital architecture we use was built as if it were not.

Every application creates its own version of the world.

Its person.
Its project.
Its client.
Its document.

Then we try to integrate everything.

Perhaps the logic should be inverted.

**Identity belongs to the reality it represents, not to the system that recorded it.**

The system should recognise that identity.

It should not appropriate it.

## Artificial intelligence makes this more urgent

Artificial intelligence changes this discussion profoundly.

For the first time, we can interrogate very large bodies of information through natural language.

This is extraordinarily powerful.

But it creates a new risk as well.

A system can produce an extremely convincing answer without being able to demonstrate how it reconstructed the reality it is describing.

Where did this fact come from?

Is it still valid?

Which document supports it?

Was it a decision or a proposal?

Who had the authority to make it?

Is there later information that supersedes it?

Without these answers, artificial memory may become little more than a very fast way of producing certainty from fragments.

This is why I believe intelligence should sit above memory rather than replace its structure.

AI can interpret.

It can connect.

It can synthesise.

It can find patterns.

It can help us ask better questions.

But the underlying reality should continue to exist in a verifiable form even when the AI model is removed.

Otherwise, we have not built memory.

We have built dependency.

## Personal memory and organisational memory

There is another boundary.

An organisation needs memory.

A person does too.

They are not the same thing.

Context that belongs to a company should not automatically become personal context.

Project information should not cross ownership boundaries simply because the same person participates in several systems.

Another distinction within DECIMA emerged from this tension.

Below, shared infrastructure capable of preserving identity, relationships, provenance and continuity.

Above, a personal layer capable of controlling which context belongs to the person, how it is used and with which systems it may be shared.

This is not only a matter of convenience.

It is a matter of sovereignty.

Memory becomes too important, once it begins to determine what a machine knows about us, to be treated merely as a software feature.

## The project should be able to tell its own story

Perhaps this is the simplest ambition.

To arrive at a project ten years from now and be able to ask:

What happened?

And for the project to be able to answer.

Not merely return documents.

Tell its evolution.

Show the decisions.
Point to the evidence.
Distinguish what was planned from what was executed.
Explain when a piece of information ceased to be valid.
Show who participated.
Preserve what changed.

A true project memory is not a collection of files.

It is the ability to reconstruct reality without depending on whoever still remembers it.

And perhaps that is what we are ultimately looking for when we speak about continuity.

Not keeping everything.

Preserving enough for the future to understand the past.

**An archive preserves information.**

**Memory preserves meaning.**
