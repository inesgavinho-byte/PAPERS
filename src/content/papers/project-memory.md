A building can contain thousands of drawings, specifications, approvals, photographs, models, technical sheets and records.

And still have no memory.

We may know where a wall is.

We may know which material was specified.

We may know which drawing is current.

But we often no longer know why that wall ended up there, why one material replaced another, which condition forced a particular change, who made that decision, or what was lost along the way.

The information exists.

The history disappears.

And that may be one of the least discussed problems in the digital transformation of architecture and construction.

For years, we have focused on storing more.

More documents.  
More data.  
More models.  
More versions.  
More records.

But storing is not remembering.

## The problem is not a lack of information

Most complex projects do not suffer from a shortage of documentation.

They suffer from fragmentation.

A drawing lives in one platform.

An approval remains in an email.

A decision was made in a meeting.

A change emerged on site.

A specification was updated in another system.

A photograph proves what was actually built.

A critical comment remains buried in a message.

Each element may still exist.

What disappears is the relationship between them.

And once that relationship is lost, so is our ability to reconstruct the project as a sequence of decisions.

This is particularly strange because a project is never just a collection of documents.

It is a temporal process.

A solution emerges.

It is studied.

Modified.

Approved.

Replaced.

Built.

Maintained.

Eventually removed.

Each state only makes sense in relation to what came before.

Yet most of our digital systems are still organised around isolated objects.

Files.

Tasks.

Messages.

Models.

Records.

Rarely around what actually produces meaning:

relationships through time.

## A project is a genealogy of decisions

We often think of a decision as a point.

In reality, it is a chain.

A material choice may begin as an aesthetic intention.

Then be constrained by performance.

Changed by availability.

Reconsidered because of cost.

Substituted because of compliance or technical requirements.

Approved by the client.

Ordered through a supplier.

Installed by a contractor.

Inspected on site.

And replaced years later during maintenance.

So what, exactly, is “the information” about that material?

The technical sheet?

The approval?

The installation photograph?

The current specification?

None of them in isolation.

The meaningful information is the relationship between all of them.

That genealogy is what turns records into knowledge.

## Project continuity should not depend on people continuity

There is another fragility we tend to accept as normal.

Projects remain intelligible while certain people remain present.

Someone knows why a decision was made.

Someone remembers which drawing became obsolete.

Someone recalls the exception.

Someone knows that a particular document does not tell the whole story.

For a while, this works.

Until it does not.

Teams change.

Consultants leave.

Contractors rotate.

Clients revisit decisions months later.

Buildings change ownership.

And what looked like organisational knowledge turns out to have been individual memory.

That is a fragile information architecture.

The continuity of a project should survive changes in the people who know it.

Not because technology should replace human memory.

But because organisations should not depend on it in order to understand what they have built.

## Traceability is not bureaucracy

The word traceability often sounds administrative.

Control.

Compliance.

Audit.

But there is a more interesting interpretation.

Traceability is the ability to understand how something became true.

Which drawing is current?

That is an operational question.

Why is this drawing current?

That is a question about memory.

The first requires access to the present state.

The second requires access to time.

It requires knowing what came before.

What changed.

Who changed it.

Which evidence supported that change.

Which decision was attached to the transition.

A genuinely traceable system does not only preserve versions.

It preserves relationships between versions.

That distinction may appear small.

It is not.

It is the difference between archive and memory.

## Project Memory

This is what I have been calling Project Memory.

It does not mean storing everything.

It does not mean turning every human interaction into a permanent record.

And it does not mean building a larger repository.

It means preserving enough context for relevant information to remain intelligible through time.

A project memory should be able to relate:

the state of something;

the period in which that state was valid;

its provenance;

the decisions that produced it;

the evidence that supported it;

its relationships with other elements;

and what later replaced it.

In simple terms:

**state + time + provenance + relationships + decisions + evidence**

Not as extra fields added to a database.

As an architecture of knowledge.

## Seeing evolution matters as much as seeing the present

Most systems privilege the current state.

That is understandable.

We need to know which drawing is valid.

Which budget is current.

Which material has been approved.

Which task is still open.

But the current state is only a photograph.

Projects are films.

When previous states are erased or hidden too quickly, a source of knowledge disappears with them.

A solution rejected today may explain a decision tomorrow.

A recurring change across several projects may reveal a process failure.

A consistent difference between what was designed and what was built may expose a pattern worth correcting.

Project history is not necessarily noise.

It can be evidence.

And accumulated evidence can become organisational learning.

## Handover should not be a transfer of files

This fragility becomes particularly visible at handover.

For years, a project accumulates decisions.

Then the building is delivered.

And much of that history is compressed into a collection of final documents.

As-built drawings.

Manuals.

Warranties.

Technical sheets.

Schedules.

Folders.

We transfer information.

But do we transfer memory?

Not always.

The owner receives the final state, but often not the genealogy that produced it.

And that distinction becomes even more important once the building enters operation.

Because a second history begins.

Equipment fails.

Materials age.

Components are replaced.

Future interventions alter previous solutions.

Regulations change.

New technologies are introduced.

The building continues to evolve.

But the digital memory accompanying it is often discontinuous.

## From Project Memory to Building Memory

This is where the idea begins to move beyond the project itself.

If information created during design and construction can preserve relationships over time, that memory can continue with the building after handover.

Not merely as an archive.

As a living structure.

Which product was installed?

Where?

When?

With which documentation?

Which version?

Who supplied it?

What warranty applies?

What maintenance does it require?

Was it later replaced?

When?

Why?

What existed before?

What exists today is the result of which sequence of decisions?

This is where the idea of a building passport becomes particularly interesting.

Not simply as an administrative record or a repository of asset characteristics.

But as a possible persistent layer of the building’s technical identity.

A building with memory.

## BIM, CDE and digital twins are not memory by themselves

We already have several important digital layers.

BIM represents.

A CDE organises.

A digital twin aims to reflect states of the asset.

A building passport can consolidate persistent information.

All of this matters.

But none of these categories guarantees memory on its own.

Because memory requires more than representation or storage.

It requires temporality.

Provenance.

Relationships.

Decisions.

Evidence.

Continuity.

It is entirely possible to have an excellent BIM model and still not know why a particular decision was made.

It is possible to have every document inside a CDE and still fail to understand the relationship between them.

It is possible to have a sophisticated digital twin and still lose the history that explains how the asset reached its current state.

Perhaps the next evolution is not another category of software.

Perhaps it is a shift in what we consider relevant information.

## Artificial intelligence makes this more urgent

AI makes this problem impossible to ignore.

It is easy to imagine systems capable of answering:

Which material was approved for this area?

Why was it changed?

Which equipment is still under warranty?

Which drawing is valid?

Which decisions affected this space?

What changed between these two versions?

But the quality of those answers will depend less on the fluency of the model than on the quality of the memory underneath it.

If data is fragmented, if relationships do not exist, if provenance has disappeared and decisions left no trace, AI cannot magically recover what was never structured.

It can infer.

It can summarise.

It can search.

It can even sound convincing.

But inference is not evidence.

And in a context where decisions have physical, economic and legal consequences, that difference matters.

An intelligent layer only becomes truly useful when it operates over a foundation that can distinguish what it knows from what it merely infers.

## Not everything should be remembered

There is also a less comfortable question.

If a building can remember, what should it remember?

Not all information deserves permanence.

Not every decision needs to survive fifty years.

Not every conversation should become part of a historical record.

Memory requires selection.

And selection implies governance.

Who decides what remains?

Who can correct it?

Who can add context?

Who can delete?

What happens when two sources conflict?

How do we distinguish a formal decision from a hypothesis?

Evidence from interpretation?

A human action from an inference produced by AI?

These questions are not peripheral.

They are part of the architecture of the system.

A serious building memory may need some of the same properties we expect from institutions that preserve history:

provenance;

responsibility;

temporal layers;

the right to correction;

and resistance to the silent rewriting of the past.

## The building as a persistent information system

Architecture has always dealt with time.

We design something that does not yet exist.

We build over years.

We use for decades.

We alter.

Adapt.

Maintain.

Demolish.

It is strange, then, that the digital information accompanying that process is still so often organised as if each moment were independent from the ones before it.

Perhaps we should begin to think of the building not only as a physical object, but also as a persistent information system.

A system whose memory begins before the first wall is built.

That grows during design.

Acquires evidence during construction.

Is transferred at handover.

And continues to evolve throughout the life of the asset.

Not to document everything obsessively.

But to make sure that what matters does not lose meaning over time.

Because buildings outlive the teams that design them.

The companies that construct them.

The tools used during the work.

And often the organisations that originally commissioned them.

Their memory should be able to do the same.
