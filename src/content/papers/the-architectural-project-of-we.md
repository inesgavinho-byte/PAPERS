Who is a building for?

The question sounds naïve, and the profession has a confident answer ready. A building is for the client. It is commissioned, paid for, and judged by the person whose programme it satisfies. Architecture, in this account, is a service: a specialised competence rented out to translate private requirement into built form. The better the translation, the better the architecture.

This is not wrong. It is merely small.

## The assumption hidden in the brief

Every commission arrives as a brief, and every brief encodes an assumption so familiar we no longer see it: that the building exists to serve the one who asked for it. The brief is written in the first person singular. It speaks of *my* house, *our* offices, *the* museum — always from the standpoint of the party with the means to commission.

But a building, once it stands, does not confine its effects to the person who paid. It casts a shadow on a neighbour's garden. It narrows or widens a street. It decides whether a passer-by feels addressed or excluded. It enters the life of a city and stays there, often for longer than the institution that built it. The client signs the cheque; everyone else signs nothing and lives with the result.

Service answers to the signatory. Architecture answers to the room.

> A building is the one purchase whose consequences are paid for, in part, by people who were never consulted.

## Service and condition

Here is the distinction the profession has blurred. To provide a *service* is to fulfil a requirement that someone has named. To make *architecture* is to create a condition that no one named, but everyone now inhabits.

A service is complete when the client is satisfied. A condition is never simply complete; it is *entered*, again and again, by people the client will never meet. The corridor that hurries you or lets you pause; the entrance that receives a stranger or repels them; the window that gives the street a face or turns a blank wall to it — these are not services. They are the terms on which a shared life will be conducted.

Hannah Arendt distinguished between the private realm of need and the public realm of appearance — the space in which we become visible to one another and a common world takes shape.[^arendt] Architecture is the manufacture of that space of appearance. It is, whether the architect intends it or not, political: it distributes the conditions under which people encounter each other.

[^arendt]: Hannah Arendt, *The Human Condition* (1958). The "space of appearance" is where, she writes, "I appear to others as others appear to me." A building is among the most durable instruments we have for fixing the terms of that appearance.

## The thesis: architecture constructs a "we"

If this is right, then the deepest project of architecture is not to serve an *I* but to construct a *we*.

Not a sentimental we — not the forced collectivity of the plaza no one crosses or the atrium designed for a photograph. The we that architecture builds is concrete and unchosen: the people who will share a wall, a stair, a street, a silence. Good architecture takes their existence seriously *before they arrive*. It treats the unnamed inhabitant — the future tenant, the eventual neighbour, the stranger seeking shelter from rain — as a party to the commission, even though they were never in the room.

This is why hospitality is not a programme type but a structural disposition. A building is hospitable when it has reserved a place for someone it cannot identify in advance. Aldo van Eyck spent a career on the *in-between* — the threshold, the doorstep, the space that belongs fully to neither inside nor out and therefore to everyone.[^vaneyck] The doorstep is the smallest unit of the we: the place where the private agrees to meet the public on terms both can bear.

[^vaneyck]: Aldo van Eyck designed the in-between as a place of reconciliation between realms — most famously in his Amsterdam orphanage and in the hundreds of playgrounds he scattered through the postwar city, each a small claim that the street belonged to its children.

## The operational principle

To design as if architecture were a service is to optimise for the signatory and let the consequences fall where they may. To design as if architecture were a condition is to accept a wider authorship — and a wider responsibility.

The principle that follows is simple to state and difficult to keep:

**Design for the inhabitant you will never meet.**

Hold a place for the stranger. Ask of every decision not only whether it satisfies the brief but what condition it leaves behind for those who never wrote one. Architecture is not the service you render to the one who asks. It is the world you leave to everyone who didn't.
