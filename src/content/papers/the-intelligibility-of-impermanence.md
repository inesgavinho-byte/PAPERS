There are things that disappear because they end.

And there are things that disappear even though they continue to exist.

A decision remains in force, but no one remembers why it was made. A building is still standing, but the history of the transformations that brought it to its present state has been lost. A document remains archived, yet it is no longer possible to understand which version it responded to, what it replaced, or which context once gave it meaning.

The information survives.

Its intelligibility does not.

This distinction has become one of the most important questions of our time.

We are surrounded by systems designed to retain. We store documents, messages, versions, images, recordings, transactions, models and data in unprecedented quantities.

In theory, we have never had so much memory.

In practice, we forget constantly.

Because memory is not accumulation.

It is continuity.



## The delicacy of the ephemeral

Japanese aesthetic tradition contains an expression that is difficult to translate without diminishing it: *mono no aware*.

It is often associated with the “beauty of impermanence”, although the idea is more subtle than that. It describes the emotion that arises from the awareness that all things are transient.

The cherry blossom has become one of its most familiar symbols.

Its beauty does not exist despite its brevity.

It exists, in part, because of it.

The knowledge that something will disappear changes the intensity with which we perceive it.

There is a profound idea here: impermanence does not necessarily diminish the value of things. It can make them more singular.

But contemporary life has introduced a different problem.

It is not simply that everything changes.

It is that we change so much, so quickly, that we often destroy our ability to understand what changed.

## Changing without losing the path

Organisations are particularly vulnerable to this problem.

A decision is made in a meeting.

Months later, only its conclusion remains in a document.

The conversation that produced it is gone.

The alternatives considered are gone.

The constraints of that moment are gone.

The people involved have changed roles or left the organisation.

Later, someone finds the decision.

But not the reasoning.

The result remains.

The path has disappeared.

This is where the distinction between preserving information and preserving intelligibility becomes essential.

An archive can tell us what existed.

A genuinely continuous system should allow us to understand how one state became another.

Not only the current state.

The genealogy of that state.

**Change ≠ Loss of Intelligibility.**

## A present without a past is ambiguous

Consider a building.

At one point, there is a drawing.

Then comes a change.

Later, a revised engineering solution, a material substitution, a site decision, an adaptation made during construction, an intervention years after completion.

Every document may still exist.

But if the relationships between them disappear, the knowledge of the building becomes fragmented.

We have information.

We do not necessarily have understanding.

This becomes especially visible decades later.

Which material was actually installed?

Why was it replaced?

Where is the correct technical sheet?

Was the change approved?

Which structural solution corresponds to what was actually built?

Which equipment was replaced?

When?

By whom?

The hardest question is rarely:

“Does a document exist?”

The question is:

“Can we reconstruct, with confidence, what actually happened?”

## A building is never truly finished

We tend to think of architecture as a succession of definitive moments: the design, the construction, the completion, the inauguration. As if a building reached, at a particular instant, its final form.

But a building is never truly finished.

Materials age. Light transforms the perception of spaces. Uses change. Inhabitants succeed one another. What was conceived to respond to certain needs inevitably comes to accommodate others.

Architecture does not exist outside time. It exists through it.

An intervention can preserve a façade and, at the same time, erase the history of the decisions that produced it. A renovation can retain material elements and eliminate the relations that gave them meaning. A building can remain physically present and become progressively less comprehensible.

That is why continuity cannot be reduced to the permanence of matter.

To understand a building implies understanding its transformations as well: what changed, when it changed, why it changed, and what relation exists between what we find today and what existed before.

During a journey to Japan, I found in traditional architecture a particularly clear expression of this continuity. I remember the long wooden corridors, the marks left on the floor, and the way the passage of time seemed to become part of the very identity of the buildings.

These were not spaces whose beauty depended on remaining untouched. On the contrary: part of their depth seemed to result from the coexistence of what had been built, what had been lived and what continued to change.

The memory of a place does not reside only in the elements that survived. It resides equally in the possibility of reconstructing its evolution.

## The paradox of digital memory

Technology has intensified this problem at the same time as it promised to solve it.

It has never been easier to store.

It has never been harder to distinguish what matters within everything we have stored.

An organisation can possess millions of messages, thousands of documents and years of digital history without possessing coherent institutional memory.

Keeping everything does not mean being able to reconstruct anything.

Even the most sophisticated search systems can fail here.

Finding a semantically similar document does not mean understanding its position within a sequence of events.

Searching for “contract”, “change”, “approval” or “façade” may return relevant information.

But relevance is not causality.

Semantic proximity is not genealogy.

And information retrieval is not necessarily knowledge.

## Permanence is not the answer

It would be tempting to conclude that we need more rigid systems.

Definitive versions.

Immutable structures.

But that answer fails precisely because reality does not work that way.

Buildings change.

Companies change.

Projects change.

People change.

Knowledge changes.

The objective should not be to prevent transformation.

It should be to preserve the conditions that allow us to understand it.

The question, then, is not how to make the world permanent.

It is how to make change intelligible.

## Another kind of memory

Perhaps we need to reconsider what we mean by digital memory.

Not as a container into which we deposit the past.

But as a structure capable of preserving relationships.

Between a decision and the information that supported it.

Between one version and the version it replaced.

Between a change and the person who authorised it.

Between an object and the succession of states it has passed through.

Between what we knew at a particular moment and what we discovered later.

From this perspective, memory no longer means preservation alone.

It becomes the possibility of reconstruction.

And that changes fundamentally how we design systems.

## The value of the trace

There is a contemporary tendency to hide complexity.

We want simple interfaces.

Immediate answers.

A single result.

A clean truth.

But reality rarely reaches us in that form.

What we know is often the product of revisions, conflicts, uncertainty, rejected hypotheses, corrections and successive decisions.

Erasing that path may produce visual simplicity.

It can also produce epistemic fragility.

Because when a result is separated from its history, we lose the ability to judge how much confidence we should place in it.

The trace is not noise.

Sometimes it is precisely what makes a result verifiable.

## Impermanence and intelligibility

*Mono no aware* invites us to recognise the transience of things.

A contemporary technological response does not need to deny that transience.

It can begin by accepting it.

Systems will change.

Documents will change.

Organisations will change.

Buildings will change.

Knowledge will change.

The problem is not that things change.

It is that, in changing them, we destroy the conditions necessary to understand how they came to be what they are.

Perhaps true memory is not the preservation of what once existed.

Perhaps it is the preservation of our ability to reconstruct what happened.

And perhaps that distinction will become increasingly important in a world that has never produced so much information — or changed so quickly.
