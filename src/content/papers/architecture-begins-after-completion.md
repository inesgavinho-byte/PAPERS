A building is not finished when construction ends. It begins when life starts testing it.

The completion certificate, the photographs, the inauguration — these mark the end of one process and are routinely mistaken for the end of the project. But the building has not yet done anything. It has only been made ready to be used, and use is where architecture is actually decided.

## The fallacy of the finished object

We photograph buildings empty. We celebrate them clean, unworn, unoccupied — at the precise moment they are least themselves. The image we keep is of a thing that has not yet been lived in, which is to say, not yet a building at all.

A building's real character emerges later: in how a corridor behaves at the end of a long day, in where people actually choose to gather, in what wears and what endures, in which intentions survive contact with use.

> Completion is a hypothesis. Occupation is the experiment.

## Time as a material

Architects work with stone, light, structure. But the most demanding material is time. A design encodes a set of bets about the future — about how people will move, what they will need, how the climate and the institution will change.

Most of these bets are invisible at completion. They are paid out slowly, over years, in maintenance and adaptation and the small daily verdicts of the people who use the space.

## The responsibility that does not end

If architecture begins after completion, then the architect's responsibility does not end there either. It extends into the life of the building:

- to design for **change**, not only for the opening day;
- to make maintenance **legible**, so the building can be cared for;
- to accept that the **best buildings age into themselves** rather than away from an original image.

To take this seriously is to design more humbly and more durably — for the long, ordinary life of a place rather than for the brief, exceptional moment of its unveiling.
