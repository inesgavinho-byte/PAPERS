Context has become one of the most frequently used words in contemporary technology.

We say that artificial intelligence needs more context.

That a team lost context.

That a decision was taken out of context.

That a platform should know the user better.

But we often use the word as a synonym for quantity.

More documents.

More history.

More messages.

More data pushed into the system.

That is not necessarily context.

It may simply be more information.

**Context ≠ Quantity.**

## A sentence never arrives alone

“Go ahead.”

Two words may appear perfectly clear.

But clear in relation to what?

A proposal?

A purchase?

A project change?

A payment?

A version that has already been superseded?

Who said it?

To whom?

When?

With what authority?

What was being discussed at that moment?

An isolated sentence contains words.

Context contains the relationships that allow us to know what those words mean.

## Context is relational

I do not think of context as a container into which information is poured.

It is a structure of relationships.

A person in relation to an organisation.

A message in relation to a decision.

A document in relation to a version.

A statement in relation to its source.

An obligation in relation to whoever assumed it.

An event in relation to the time in which it occurred.

A question in relation to what we actually need to know.

The same fragment of information can mean something different when one of these relationships changes.

This is why context is not merely semantic proximity.

It is position within a reality.

## Time changes meaning

Context is temporal too.

A decision may have been valid in May and ceased to be valid in June.

A price may be correct on the date of a proposal and wrong at the moment of purchase.

A person may have authority today and may not have had it when a message was sent.

A specification may have been official until the moment another replaced it.

If time is removed, two apparently contradictory statements may look like an error when they actually describe successive states of the same reality.

Or the opposite: two apparently compatible statements may belong to moments that can no longer be combined.

Context does not answer only “what?”

It also answers “when?”

## Origin changes the weight of a statement

Context includes provenance.

A conclusion does not matter only because of its content.

It also matters because we can know where it came from.

Was it directly observed?

Was it reported by someone?

Was it inferred?

Was it calculated?

Was it approved?

Was it merely suggested?

Provenance allows us to distinguish information from evidence, and evidence from authority.

Without that distinction, a system may know many things and still not know which of them can support a decision.

## The question is part of the context too

There is another dimension that is often ignored.

Context does not depend only on reality.

It depends on the question.

“How much did it cost?”

“Why was it changed?”

“Who approved it?”

“What remains undecided?”

“What was actually executed?”

These questions may concern the same project and require entirely different sets of information.

Giving the system everything does not solve the problem.

It relocates it.

The machine — or the person — still has to distinguish the relevant from the irrelevant at the moment of answering.

Useful context is selective.

Not because it hides reality.

Because it respects the question.

## More context can produce less understanding

There is an almost automatic assumption that more context always improves an answer.

It does not.

More context can introduce old versions.

It can bring together sources with different authority.

It can mix facts with interpretations.

It can bury decisive evidence among hundreds of irrelevant elements.

It can increase volume without increasing intelligibility.

The problem then stops being access to information.

It becomes architecture.

Which relationships should be preserved?

Which boundaries should not be crossed?

What is relevant to this question?

What needs to remain visible because it contradicts the dominant narrative?

What do we still not know?

## This is one of the problems I began working on in DECIMA

An important part of the work I have been developing in DECIMA begins precisely with this distinction.

I am not interested in building a system that can merely ingest more information.

I am interested in preserving enough continuity for information, evidence, people, decisions and events to be understood through the relationships that give them meaning.

That led me to think of context as an architectural property.

Not as a text window.

Not as a larger folder.

Not as the indiscriminate memory of everything that passed through a system.

But as a disciplined way of bringing a question closer to the relevant part of reality without losing provenance, time or the possibility of verification.

The concrete implementation of this idea belongs to the proprietary architecture I am developing.

The principle, however, can be stated publicly:

**an intelligent system does not need to know everything at the same time; it needs to be able to reach the right context without losing its connection to the reality that supports it.**

## THREAD showed me the problem through communication

In THREAD, this question appears in a particularly concrete form.

An inbox contains thousands of messages.

An organisation does not need thousands of messages.

It needs to understand relationships.

Which message belongs to which project?

Which person represents which entity?

Which communication changes what we knew?

Which reply merely repeats earlier information?

Which commitment emerges from a conversation?

Which message only makes sense when connected to a document or a decision?

The value is not in placing all email inside an artificial intelligence system.

It is in preventing the channel through which information arrived from becoming the prison of its meaning.

## Context is also a question of boundaries

If context is powerful, it can also be dangerous.

Not everything a system is capable of knowing should be available in every situation.

An organisation may have legitimate access to particular information in a professional context without thereby acquiring unlimited rights over a person's private context.

A team may need to understand a decision without needing access to every private communication of the people involved.

An agent may need to execute a task without receiving the entire memory of a person or organisation.

More access is not automatically more intelligence.

Sometimes it is simply more exposure.

A serious architecture of context therefore needs to know how to include.

And it also needs to know how to exclude.

## Context should not belong to the model

There is another technical and philosophical consequence.

If an organisation's context exists only inside the memory of a particular artificial-intelligence model, the organisation has become dependent on that model.

If we change provider, do we lose context?

If we switch AI off, do the relationships cease to exist?

If a model misinterprets a sequence, does reality change?

It should not.

Intelligence can use context.

It can interpret it.

It can search relationships.

It can synthesise it.

But the structure that allows that context to be reconstructed should remain independent of whichever model is reading it at the time.

It is the difference between using artificial intelligence over an organised reality and asking artificial intelligence to become reality itself.

## The right context is not an answer

Even well-structured context does not eliminate judgement.

It can make a situation more intelligible.

It can reveal contradictions.

It can show the origin of a claim.

It can recover what happened before.

It can prevent an old version from being presented as current.

But it does not automatically turn a difficult question into an objective one.

Some decisions still require responsibility.

Some conflicts do not disappear when we have more information.

Some choices involve values, risk and intention.

A good architecture of context does not replace judgement.

It gives judgement better conditions.

## From information to meaning

Perhaps context is precisely this:

the structure that allows information to acquire meaning without losing the possibility of returning to what supports it.

Relationships.

Time.

Origin.

Authority.

Purpose.

Boundaries.

State.

One question may require only a small part of this structure.

Another may require a much wider reading.

Rigour does not come from always providing more.

It comes from providing enough — and showing where it came from.

**Context is not what fits inside a window.**

**It is the architecture that makes reality intelligible.**
